I really like to swim. I'm definitely not a professional athlete, but I've spent enough time in the pool to realize that if you want to improve your technique, simply counting laps isn't going to cut it. 

Don't get me wrong, the native Apple Watch Workout app is fantastic for the big picture. It tracks your total distance, your average heart rate, and even calculates your SWOLF score perfectly. But when it comes to the micro-level, the biomechanical breakdown of your stroke, it falls completely short. 

The Apple Watch hardware itself is incredibly powerful and captures more than enough high-fidelity data for deep biomechanical analysis. However, the native Fitness app is designed for a general consumer audience. It abstracts away all that raw sensor data, choosing instead to present only the high-level summaries. Because of this, you can't see your stroke-by-stroke power decay over a set, or precise arm turnover rates.

You might be thinking, "Why not just use a Sensor Logger app?" While they do exist, they are almost always hidden behind a paywall and, more importantly, they are rarely optimized for the wet environment of a swimming pool.

So, I decided to build my own solution.

## The Idea: A Custom Apple Watch Streamer

<p align="center"><img src="https://storage.googleapis.com/blog-images-southamerica-east1/2026/07/streaming-apple-watch-imu/app-icon.png" alt="SwimDataStreamer App Icon featuring a stylized swimmer" /></p>

The goal was simple: build a lightweight Apple Watch app that extracts real-time Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) data, specifically tailored for swimming.

For those unfamiliar, an IMU is an electronic component packed with accelerometers and gyroscopes. It measures a body's specific force and angular rate. Inside the Apple Watch, the IMU is the invisible engine powering features like step counting, fall detection, and wrist-raise detection. By tapping directly into this raw data stream, we can measure the exact physical forces (like gravity and acceleration) generated by every single arm stroke in the water.

![SwimDataStreamer Apple Watch and iOS Companion App UI Mockups](https://storage.googleapis.com/blog-images-southamerica-east1/2026/07/streaming-apple-watch-imu/swim_app_ui.png)

To pull this off, I tapped into the HealthKit interface, hooking into the Workout Session API. This lets the app run seamlessly in the background during a swim workout while pulling high-frequency sensor data like User Acceleration (`userAcc`), Rotation Rate (`rotation`), and Heart Rate. 

One of the biggest hurdles when building anything for swimming is the water itself. Capacitive screens go crazy when wet, causing ghost touches that can accidentally pause or ruin your recording. To fix this, I made sure the app automatically locks the watch screen the second the workout starts. 

Since Bluetooth doesn't travel through water, streaming the data live to a phone sitting on the pool deck wasn't an option. Instead, the data is buffered and stored locally on the watch. Once I finish my sets, I press the Digital Crown and Side Button to unlock the screen, end the workout, and the raw CSV file automatically syncs over to my iPhone for offline analysis.

## Making Sense of the Raw Data

Getting the data is only half the battle. Once it's synced to the phone, the real fun begins. I wrote a Python script to chew through the raw CSV, extract the active swimming blocks, and plot both the biomechanical and cardiovascular responses.

![Session Block Analysis Dashboard showing stroke power and arm turnover rate](https://storage.googleapis.com/blog-images-southamerica-east1/2026/07/streaming-apple-watch-imu/dashboard.png)

When you visualize it, you get a completely different perspective on your workout:

*   **Stroke Power:** By looking at the `userAcc` vectors, you can see the exact G-force of each pull. It’s a direct proxy for the power you generate with every single stroke.
*   **Wrist Orientation & Arm Turnover:** The gravity vector and rotation rate act as a precise proxy for your form and stroke rate. You can literally see how your arm is moving through the water.

The biggest insight comes when you compare different sets. In the visualization above, Sets 1-6 were clearly high-intensity sprints. They are short, with massive peak efforts (hitting up to 8.27 G!), and because they were so brief, the average heart rate stayed relatively low. 

On the flip side, Sets 7-10 were endurance pulls. They lasted much longer (around 3:55), had a lower peak effort per stroke, but kept my heart rate sustained up to 155 BPM. Seeing this micro-data is a game-changer. I can spot the exact moment my stroke power starts dropping off due to fatigue during an endurance set, which tells me exactly when I need to focus harder on maintaining my form.

## Try It Out

Because this is a highly specialized, experimental tool, I haven't bothered jumping through the hoops to deploy it to the App Store. But if you have a Mac with Xcode, you can easily compile and deploy it locally to your own devices.

All the source code, including the Watch and iOS companion apps, is open and available on GitHub:
[SwimDataStreamer Repository](https://github.com/hvini/SwimDataStreamer)

It bridges the gap between basic fitness tracking and actual sports science. Next up, I plan to redesign the iOS companion app to include this data visualization dashboard directly in the app, so I won't have to rely on a separate Python script for the analysis. Down the line, maybe I'll even add real-time haptic feedback on the watch if my stroke rate drops below a certain threshold!
